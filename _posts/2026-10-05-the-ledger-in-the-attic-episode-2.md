By the time I got my coat on and pushed through Owen's front door, the gray truck was already moving.

It rolled out of the far end of the lot slowly, almost lazily, the way you'd leave a place you knew you'd be coming back to. I ran a few steps after it like an idiot, my scrubs flapping, the envelope pressed against my chest. The license plate was caked in mud. I couldn't read a single number.

Then it turned onto Route 9 and was gone.

I stood there in the cold, breathing hard, until Owen appeared in the doorway behind me.

"Did you see who it was?" he called.

"No." I turned around. "Owen, who would be watching me? Watching *your office*?"

He looked out at the empty road for a long moment, and for the first time all morning, he looked his age.

"Go somewhere public," he said. "Somewhere with people. And Nora? Do what she asked. Don't open it at the house."

I went to Dot's Diner, because it was the most public place I could think of and because I hadn't eaten since a granola bar at two in the morning.

Dot's hadn't changed since I was a kid. Red vinyl booths patched with tape, a pie case that hummed, and Dot herself, who was eighty and still worked the register. She took one look at me and brought coffee without asking.

"Sorry about Evie, honey," she said, and squeezed my shoulder. "She used to sit right there every Thursday. Patty melt, no onions, extra pickles. Fifty years."

"I know," I said. My throat closed up. "Thank you, Dot."

I waited until she'd gone back to the register. Then I sat in the corner booth with my back to the wall, where I could see the door and the parking lot, and I broke the red wax seal.

Something small and heavy slid out onto the table with a clunk.

A key.

Old brass, darkened with age, longer than my finger, with a loop of faded yellow yarn tied through the bow. I knew that yarn. It was the same butter-yellow wool Grandma had used to knit the blanket on the back of her rocking chair, the one I'd fallen asleep under a thousand times as a kid.

Underneath the key was a single sheet of her lavender stationery, folded twice.

I unfolded it. My hands were shaking so badly I had to flatten it on the table to read it.

*My darling Nora,*

*If you are reading this, then Raymond stood up first and your mother left last, and Owen has given you this letter with that sad look he gets. I'm sorry, sweetheart. I wish I could have spared you that room.*

*There is a ledger in the attic at the lake house. It is the most important thing I own, more than the store, more than the house. This key opens the attic. There is only one other, and it is not in this family.*

*Find the ledger before Ray does.*

*Don't trust the store's books. Don't tell your mother yet. Not because she is wicked, but because she has been hurt in a place I put there myself, a long time ago, and she will need time.*

*You were always the brave one. Even when you were small and afraid of the dark, you'd still go down to the dock to check the boat was tied. Be brave a little while longer.*

*All my love, always,*
*Grandma x*

The *x* after her name was the tiny cross-stitch she put on everything: birthday cards, grocery lists, the notes she left in my lunchbox in third grade. I touched it with my fingertip, and that was when I finally cried, right there in Dot's corner booth, with my coffee going cold and a stranger's key in my hand.

*Not in this family.* What did that mean?

And why, of all the things Grandma owned, would a ledger matter more than the store she'd spent fifty years building?

I picked Lily up from school at three-fifteen.

My daughter is twelve, with my dark curly hair and her father's glasses, and she treats her phone like a documentary crew. She was filming the parking lot when I pulled up, narrating to nobody in particular.

"And here we see the Honda, approaching at a cautious speed," she said, sliding into the back seat. "The driver appears to have been crying. Again."

"Thank you, David Attenborough."

"Who?"

"Never mind." I pulled out into traffic. "How was school?"

"Fine. How was the will thing?" She leaned forward between the seats. "Did Grandma Evie leave you the store? Uncle Ray said at the funeral she was going to leave it to him. He said it really loud. Like, *really* loud."

I glanced at her in the mirror. Lily notices everything. It's both the best and most exhausting thing about her.

"She left me the store," I said carefully. "And the lake house."

Lily's mouth fell open. "The *lake house*? Mom! We can go swimming every day in the summer!"

"It's complicated, Lil."

"Why?"

Because your great-uncle looked at me like he wanted to put me through a wall. Because your grandmother called me a vulture with her eyes. Because somebody in a gray truck was watching me this morning, and there is a key in my coat pocket that I'm afraid to touch.

"Grown-up stuff," I said.

Lily sighed the deepest sigh available to a twelve-year-old and went back to filming the window.

I meant to wait until morning.

I really did. I made spaghetti. I helped Lily with her fractions. I folded laundry and watched the news without hearing a word of it. But every time I passed my coat on the hook by the door, I could feel that key in the pocket like it was warm.

*Find the ledger before Ray does.*

At nine-thirty, I gave up.

"Get your shoes on," I told Lily. "We're going for a drive."

She looked up from the couch, instantly suspicious. "Where?"

"Grandma Evie's."

"At *night*?"

"I need to check on something. You'll stay in the car with the doors locked. That's the deal."

I should have called Greg to take her. I know that now. But Greg was on the late shift at the warehouse, my mother wasn't speaking to me, and I wasn't about to leave my daughter alone in an apartment while a gray truck drove around town. So I bundled her into her hoodie, and we drove the twenty minutes out to Pine Hollow Lake with the radio off.

The lake house sits at the end of a long gravel lane, tucked into the pines with the water behind it. Grandma lived there full-time for the last eight years of her life, after the stairs in her town house got to be too much. In daylight, it's the most peaceful place I know. At night, with no porch light and the trees leaning over the lane, it looked like something holding its breath.

I parked by the old woodshed, facing the lane.

"Doors locked," I said. "Phone charged?"

"Seventy-two percent."

"If anything happens, anything at all, you lean on that horn and you don't stop. Understand?"

Lily's face went from bored to serious in a heartbeat. "Mom. What's going on?"

"Nothing," I lied. "I just need to grab something of Grandma's. Five minutes."

She held up her phone. "I'm timing you."

The front door key was still on my ring, the one Grandma gave me three years ago when I started coming every morning. The lock turned easily. I stepped inside and flipped the hall light.

Nothing happened.

I flipped it again. Dead. I tried the lamp on the side table, the one with the fringe shade she'd had since the seventies. Dead too.

The breaker, I told myself. Old house. It happens.

I turned on my phone's flashlight. The beam slid across the front room: her rocking chair with the yellow blanket folded over the back, her knitting basket, the bookshelf crammed with paperback mysteries. It smelled like her. Lavender and coffee and the faint, dusty sweetness of old wool. For a second I just stood there and breathed it in, and I could almost hear her calling from the kitchen, *Is that my girl?*

Then I heard the floor creak above me.

I froze.

It's an old house, I told myself again. Old houses settle. The wood contracts when it gets cold.

The creak came again. Slow. Deliberate. The particular groan of the third board from the top of the attic stairs, the one Grandma always told me to skip when I was small so she wouldn't hear me sneaking up to try the door.

Somebody was skipping nothing. Somebody was standing on it.

My heart was slamming so hard I could feel it in my teeth. Every sensible part of me said *get out, get in the car, drive*. But Lily was in that car, and whoever was upstairs was between me and whatever Grandma had wanted me to find, and somewhere under the fear there was a hot, stubborn little coal of anger.

This was her house. *My* house now.

"Hello?" I called. My voice came out higher than I wanted. "Whoever's up there, I've called the police."

I hadn't. But I pulled up the keypad with my thumb as I said it.

For a long, terrible second, there was nothing. Then everything happened at once: quick, heavy footsteps overhead, the rattle of the back stairs, and from the kitchen, the sharp *slap* of the screen door banging shut.

I ran to the kitchen window just in time to see a dark shape cut across the backyard toward the lake path and disappear into the pines. Big. Fast. That was all I could tell. A flashlight beam bobbed once between the trees and went out.

Somewhere down by the water, an engine started.

I stood at the window with my hand over my mouth until I couldn't hear it anymore.

Then I made myself climb the stairs.

The attic door is at the top of a narrow staircase off the upstairs hall, and for as long as I can remember it's been locked with an old brass padlock as big as my fist. Grandma's one rule in a house with no rules. *That's my room, Nora-bug. Everybody needs one room that's just theirs.*

I pointed my flashlight at it.

The padlock was still closed. But the brass all around the keyhole was covered in fresh, bright scratches, deep silver gouges through fifty years of dark patina, like someone had been working at it with a screwdriver or a pick. There were curls of shaved metal on the floorboards, fine as hair.

I crouched and touched one. It was still sharp.

Whoever had been up here had been at that lock for a long time. And they'd left in a hurry, because they'd dropped something on the top step.

I picked it up and turned it over in the beam of my flashlight.

It was a butterscotch candy, still in its gold cellophane wrapper.

Exactly like the ones in Owen Pratt's bowl.

Down in the driveway, Lily started leaning on the horn.
