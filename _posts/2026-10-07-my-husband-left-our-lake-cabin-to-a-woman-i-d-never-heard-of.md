"To Danielle Price," the lawyer read, "I leave the cabin on Indian Lake, its contents, and the balance of my savings account at Fifth Third Bank."

I waited for the rest of the sentence. The part where my husband explained himself.

There wasn't one.

Mr. Abernathy cleared his throat and turned the page, and I sat there in his leather chair with my purse on my knees, trying to remember if I had ever, in forty-eight years of marriage, heard Frank Whitaker say the name Danielle.

I hadn't.

---

My name is Joan Whitaker. I'm sixty-nine. I married Frank in the summer of 1978, in my mother's backyard in Dayton, Ohio, under a borrowed tent that leaked when it rained. Which it did, all afternoon. Frank stood under the drip with his tie soaked through and said it was good luck. He was an electrician. He wired half the houses on our side of town, and he was the kind of man who could not walk past a crooked picture frame without straightening it.

We had two sons, four grandchildren, and a cabin on Indian Lake that Frank built mostly with his own hands over eleven summers. That cabin was the love of his life after me. Maybe before me, some Julys.

And he had left it to a stranger.

"Who is she?" I asked. My voice came out very steady, which surprised me.

Mr. Abernathy looked genuinely unhappy. "Frank asked me not to explain the bequest, Mrs. Whitaker. He said..." He glanced down at a note in the file. "He said you should ask Carol."

Carol is my younger sister.

---

I didn't drive to Carol's house right away. I drove to the cabin.

I don't fully know why. I think I wanted to stand in it one more time while it still felt like mine. It was October, and the lake was gray and still, and the cabin smelled the way it always smelled after it had been closed up: cedar and dust and the faint ghost of Frank's pipe tobacco, which he'd quit in 1996 but which had soaked into the logs forever.

I went through everything. I'm not proud of it. I opened every drawer and every cupboard and every shoebox in the closet, looking for the woman my husband had loved enough to give her the thing he loved most.

In the bedroom closet, on the top shelf, behind his fishing waders, I found an album.

Not one of ours. Ours are all at the house, labeled by year in my handwriting. This one was brown leather, worn soft at the corners, and it was full of a stranger's life. A dark-haired woman at a college graduation, squinting into the sun. The same woman at her wedding. Holding a newborn. Standing on a dock that I realized, with a slow, sick lurch, was our dock, *this* dock, with Frank beside her, his arm around her shoulders, both of them laughing.

There were birthday cards tucked into the back pocket. Dozens of them. Every one was addressed to Frank, and every one was signed the same way.

*With love, Dani.*

The newest was from last spring. *Happy 72nd. Thank you for everything, always. Still waiting for that walleye you promised me. With love, Dani.*

I sat down on the edge of the bed we'd shared for forty summers and I thought: twenty-seven years. The oldest card was from 1999. My husband had a second life for twenty-seven years, at our cabin, on our dock, while I was home in Dayton canning tomatoes.

And my sister knew.

---

Carol opened her door before I knocked. She'd seen my car in the driveway. She'd been waiting, I realized. Probably since the funeral.

She took one look at the album in my arms and put her hand over her mouth.

"Oh, Joanie," she said. "Oh, honey. He promised he'd tell you himself."

"Tell me *what*, Carol?" I pushed past her into her kitchen and dropped the album on the table so hard the sugar bowl jumped. "That he had a girlfriend? A daughter? Is that it? Is she his daughter? Did you know about this for twenty-seven years and let me sit across from you at every Christmas?"

Carol is three years younger than me, small and soft-spoken, the kind of person who apologizes to furniture when she bumps into it. She didn't apologize now. She sat down at the table very slowly and looked at me with an expression I had never seen on her face before.

"Joanie," she said. "Sit down. Please."

"I don't want to sit down."

"She's not Frank's daughter," Carol said quietly.

I stood there with my coat still on.

"Then whose is she?"

Carol reached across the table, opened the album to the first page, the college graduation, and turned it toward me.

"Look at her," she said. "Really look at her. Look at her eyes."

---

I want to explain something about the spring of 1974.

I was seventeen. I was a junior at Fairmont High, and I was in love with a boy named Gary who played trumpet in the marching band and moved to California with his family that June without leaving a forwarding address. By the time I understood why I was sick every morning, he was gone.

In 1974, in our parish, in our family, there was only one thing to do. My parents told everyone I had mono and was going to stay with our Aunt Ruth in Lexington to recover. I stayed with Aunt Ruth for seven months. I gave birth at a Catholic hospital in December, and a nun with cold hands took my daughter away before I could hold her. I saw her for maybe ten seconds. She had a full head of dark hair. That's all I knew about her for fifty-two years.

I came home in January, finished high school, and never spoke of it again. Not to my friends. Not to my parents, who never brought it up either. Not to Frank, whom I met three years later at a church picnic and married a year after that.

Especially not to Frank.

Carol was fourteen that year. She was the only one who knew where I'd really gone, because she found my letters to Aunt Ruth. She kept my secret for fifty-two years without ever once mentioning it.

Or so I thought.

---

"In 1999," Carol said, "a woman called my house."

I had finally sat down. My coat was still on. The album was open between us.

"She was twenty-four. She'd been adopted as a baby in Kentucky, and she'd spent two years trying to find her birth mother. The adoption agency had closed, but she found an old letter in her file with Aunt Ruth's address. Aunt Ruth had died by then, but a neighbor remembered there'd been a niece. And she found me." Carol twisted her napkin. "She thought I was you, at first."

"Why didn't you tell me?" I whispered.

"Because I was scared, Joanie. You'd spent twenty-five years pretending it never happened. Every time anyone on TV even mentioned adoption, you'd leave the room. I didn't know what it would do to you." Carol's eyes filled. "So I did a terrible thing. I didn't tell you. I told Frank."

I stared at her.

"I thought he'd know what to do," she said. "He was the steadiest person I'd ever met. I thought he'd find a gentle way to tell you."

"And instead?"

"Instead he drove to Kentucky to meet her," Carol said. "Just to make sure she was a good person before he brought her into your life. And she was. She was the kindest girl, Joanie. A schoolteacher. And Frank came back and he tried to tell you. He told me he tried so many times. Do you remember the summer he kept bringing up adoption at dinner? Asking what you thought about it, in general?"

I did remember. Summer of 1999. Frank asking strange questions over pot roast. *Do you ever think about families who give up babies, Joanie? Do you think the mothers ever want to be found?*

And I remembered what I'd said. I had said it more than once, because he kept asking.

*Some doors are meant to stay closed, Frank. If I'd given up a baby, I'd want her to have a whole life without me in it. I'd never want to be found.*

I'd said it to protect myself. I'd had no idea I was talking to someone who already knew.

"He thought you meant it," Carol said gently. "He didn't want to force that door open. But he couldn't stand the idea of her having nobody from your side, either. So he became her family instead. Quietly. He taught her to fish. He walked her down the aisle when her adoptive father was too sick to do it. He drove to Kentucky when her son was born." She touched a photo of the dock. "He always said that one day, when you were ready, he'd bring her to the cabin and introduce you. He kept thinking there'd be more time."

I couldn't speak. I looked down at the photograph of the young woman at her college graduation, squinting into the sun, and I saw what I had refused to see at the cabin.

She was squinting with *my* eyes. Narrow and gray-green, crinkled at the corners. My mother's eyes. My eyes.

---

Mr. Abernathy had one more thing for me, it turned out. A sealed letter, to be delivered only if I asked Carol. Frank had thought of everything. Of course he had.

It was short. Frank never used ten words when four would do.

*Joanie,*

*I'm sorry. I should have told you in 1999. I was a coward about it because I thought I was protecting you, and I think now I was only protecting myself from seeing you hurt.*

*I couldn't make you open that door while I was alive. Maybe I can make you walk through it now. The cabin was always going to be hers someday anyway. I just wanted you two to meet on that dock.*

*She is wonderful. She is so much like you, it scares me sometimes.*

*All my love, Frank.*

*P.S. The walleye are biting off the north point in October. Tell her I'm sorry about the one I promised.*

---

I called Danielle Price on a Sunday afternoon, with Carol sitting next to me holding my hand. I had written down what I was going to say on an index card. I didn't use any of it.

She picked up on the second ring. "Hello?"

I opened my mouth and nothing came out.

There was a long pause on the line. And then, very softly, a voice I had never heard but somehow recognized said:

"Joan? Is that you?"

I started to cry. On the other end of the line, so did she.

"He said you'd call someday," Danielle said through tears. "He was so sure. I told him he was a hopeless optimist."

"He was," I managed. "He was the most hopeless optimist I ever met."

---

We met at the cabin three weeks later. I got there early and stood on the dock with my hands in my coat pockets, shaking so hard I had to hold the railing.

Her car came down the gravel road at exactly noon. She got out. She was fifty-one years old, with gray streaks in her dark hair and reading glasses pushed up on her head, and when she saw me on the dock, she stopped walking.

We just looked at each other across the yard for a long moment.

Then she smiled, and her eyes crinkled at the corners, and I was seventeen years old again in a cold hospital room, reaching out my arms for ten seconds before a nun took my baby away.

This time nobody took her.

---

Danielle tried to give the cabin back. She tried very hard. She said it was mine, that Frank had built it for me, that she couldn't possibly accept it.

I told her Frank had never once in his life done anything by accident.

So we share it now. She brings her husband and her son, Owen, who is twenty and has Frank's habit of straightening crooked picture frames, which nobody can explain. My boys come up with their families on the long weekends. The first time all of us sat around the fire pit together, my oldest son looked across the flames at his half-sister and said, "Mom, she laughs exactly like you," and Danielle and I both laughed at the same time, which only proved his point.

Carol comes up too. She cried for the whole first weekend. I told her I'd forgiven her for 1999. She said she hadn't forgiven herself yet. I told her she had plenty of time.

Last October, Danielle and I took Frank's old aluminum boat out to the north point at dawn. The lake was gray and still, exactly the way it had been the day I found the album. We didn't talk much. We didn't need to.

At a quarter to seven, her line went tight.

It was a walleye. A beauty, almost five pounds. She held it up in the cold morning light, laughing, with my eyes and Frank's patience, and I took her picture for the brown leather album that sits on the cabin shelf now, right next to ours.

"That's the one he promised me," she said.

"Then he kept his promise," I said. "He always did. Eventually."
