The first thing my sister said to me at our mother's house, three weeks after the funeral, was, "Don't touch anything. I've photographed every room."

She was standing in the front hallway in a camel coat that probably cost more than my car payment, holding her phone up like evidence. Corinne is four years older than me. We hadn't spoken in almost two years, not since a Thanksgiving that ended with a thrown gravy boat and my nephew crying in the car.

"Good morning to you too, Cora," I said.

"I mean it, Tessa. The lawyer said we split everything fifty-fifty, and I'm not letting things walk out the door in your trunk."

I laughed, but it came out ugly. "That's rich. You're the one who wants to sell it to the first developer who waves a check."

"Because I can't afford to sit on a house in Halbrook for sentimental reasons. Some of us don't have that luxury."

That word, *luxury*, did something to the back of my neck. I'd spent eleven years in a one-bedroom apartment above a laundromat, working double shifts at the dental office after my divorce. Corinne had a husband with a pension and a house with a guest room nobody slept in. Our mother had always seemed softer with her, always calling her first, always asking about the grandkids.

"You had every luxury," I said. "Mom practically bankrolled your life."

Her face went white, then red. "Excuse me? I never took a dime from her. You're the one who was always crying poor on the phone. Don't think she didn't tell me."

"She didn't tell you anything, because there was nothing to tell."

That was a lie, and I knew it as I said it. I just didn't know yet that we were both lying.

---

We spent the morning in a cold war, room by room. The lawyer had told us to make an inventory and agree on the house before anything got listed. Corinne wanted it on the market by spring. I wanted to buy her out, though I had no idea how I'd ever qualify for that loan.

Mom's house was small and yellow, with a porch that sagged on the left side and a kitchen that still smelled faintly of cinnamon and dish soap. Every drawer was full of rubber bands and coupons and twist ties she'd saved "just in case." She'd lived through hard years when we were kids, after Dad left, and she never stopped living like the hard years might come back.

I was in the kitchen, sorting the pantry, when I found the recipe box.

It was on the top shelf behind the canning jars. It was a dented green tin with a little brass lock on the front, the kind you could probably open with a hairpin if you were patient. I remembered it from childhood. Mom kept her recipes in it, and she never let us touch it. "That's my brain in there," she used to say. "You'll get it when I'm gone."

I took it down and turned it over. Something shifted inside, heavier than index cards.

"What's that?" Corinne was in the doorway.

"Mom's recipe box."

She crossed the kitchen in three steps. "She promised that to me."

"She promised it to both of us. She said 'you'll get it,' and she said it to both of us at the same time, Cora. I was there."

"She told me privately. When Dylan was born. She said I was the one who actually cooked."

"Oh, so now I can't cook?"

We were both holding it. I'm not proud of that. Two grown women, one forty-one and one forty-five, playing tug-of-war over a dented tin box in their dead mother's kitchen. My sister's knuckles were white. Mine probably were too.

"Let go," she said.

"You let go."

The tin slipped. It hit the linoleum with a clang, and the lid popped up half an inch before the lock caught. Through the gap I saw it wasn't recipe cards on top.

It was envelopes.

---

We stood there breathing hard. Then, very slowly, Corinne knelt and picked up the box.

"There's no key," she said quietly.

I looked around the kitchen, and something surfaced from thirty years back. Mom at this counter, handing me a spoon, saying, *The sweet stuff's always at the bottom, Tessie. You have to dig for it.*

I went to the counter, took down the old ceramic sugar canister shaped like a fat hen, and pushed my fingers through the sugar. At the very bottom, wrapped in a twist of wax paper, was a tiny brass key.

Corinne stared at me. "How did you know that?"

"I didn't. I just remembered something she used to say."

She held out the box. For the first time that day, she didn't look like she wanted to fight. She looked scared.

I unlocked it.

The recipe cards were there in the back, soft and stained with grease. In front of them, filed behind a divider Mom had labeled *THE GIRLS* in her careful slanted print, were two thick bundles of envelopes held with rubber bands. One said *Corinne.* One said *Tessa.*

Between them was a small spiral notebook.

I opened the notebook first. It was a ledger, two columns, dates going back eleven years.

*March — T — $450 to Mr. Dimas (her landlord).*
*March — C — $450 to Westbrook Property.*
*April — T — $450.*
*April — C — $450.*

Month after month, page after page. The numbers crept up as the years went on: $500, then $550, then $600. Always the same for both of us. Never once uneven.

Corinne made a small sound, like someone had pressed on a bruise.

"Westbrook," she whispered. "That's our building. That's where we lived before we bought the house."

I couldn't speak. Because Mr. Dimas was my landlord. Eleven years ago, when my ex-husband cleaned out our joint account and left me with nothing, Mom had driven two hours to my apartment, sat on my sagging couch, and said, *Let me help with the rent. Just until you're on your feet. And Tessa, don't tell your sister. She'll think I play favorites, and she's already so sure I do.*

I'd been so ashamed that I made her promise not to tell Corinne either. I never got fully on my feet, and she never stopped paying.

I looked at my sister. Tears were running down her face.

"Gary got laid off," she said. "The year Dylan was born. We were going to lose the apartment. Mom said she'd cover half. She said—" Her voice broke. "She said, *Don't tell Tessa. She's struggling enough without thinking I've got money to throw around.*"

We just looked at each other across the kitchen.

All those years I'd hated her for being the favorite. All those years she'd hated me for the same thing. And the whole time, our mother had been splitting herself perfectly in half.

---

The letters were dated by year. Mom had written one to each of us every Christmas and never mailed them. Corinne read hers at the table. I read mine standing at the sink, because I couldn't sit still.

The last one, dated just four months before she died, was the same for both of us. She must have written it twice, word for word.

*My darling girl,*

*If you are reading this, I'm gone, and I would bet my good roasting pan that you and your sister have been fighting over this house. Don't. It's just a house. The roof leaks over the back bedroom and the porch is tired.*

*I want you to know something I promised not to tell you. I've been helping your sister too. The same amount, every month, the same as you. I never gave one of you a penny I didn't give the other. Not because you both needed exactly the same, but because I knew someday you'd hold it up to the light and compare, and I wanted you to find it even.*

*I let you each think you were the only one I was worried about. That was wrong of me, maybe. But you were both so proud, and I thought if you knew the other one was struggling, you'd each feel like you had to be the strong one. You'd stop calling each other. You stopped anyway. That's the part that hurt.*

*I didn't have much. I sold Grandma's ring. I stopped buying the good coffee. I didn't mind, not one day of it. It was the best money I ever spent.*

*The house is yours, together. Do something good with it. And call your sister.*

*All my love, always,*
*Mom*

*P.S. The pot roast card is in the back. Use more garlic than it says. I always lied about the garlic.*

---

Corinne laughed first. It was a wet, broken laugh, but it was real. "She lied about the garlic. I *knew* mine never tasted right."

"Neither did mine."

She wiped her face with the sleeve of her expensive coat. "I said such awful things to you."

"I said worse."

"The gravy boat."

"I still maintain it slipped."

She laughed again, and then she was crying, and then I was too, and somehow we ended up sitting on the kitchen floor with our backs against the cabinets, the recipe box between us, the way we used to sit when we were kids waiting for cookies to come out of the oven.

---

We didn't sell the house.

We fixed the leak over the back bedroom and the tired porch with our own hands over six long weekends. Gary helped. My nephew Dylan, who's fourteen now and taller than both of us, carried lumber and complained the entire time. It was the most I'd seen of my sister's family in years.

Then we rented the house out.

The woman who lives there now is named Priya. She's twenty-nine, newly divorced, with a four-year-old daughter who calls the porch swing "the flying chair." She thinks her rent is low because the owners are "just nice people who don't care about money."

That's not quite true. We charge her exactly half of what the place is worth. Corinne covers half the difference and I cover the other half, to the dollar. We keep a little spiral notebook on Mom's kitchen counter, two columns, every month even.

Priya doesn't know about the notebook. She doesn't need to.

Last Sunday, Corinne and I made the pot roast together in my apartment, which is still above the laundromat and still has the sagging couch. We used three times the garlic the card says.

It tasted exactly like home.
