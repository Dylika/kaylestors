Owen Pratt kept a glass bowl of butterscotch candies on the corner of his desk, and that is the detail I keep coming back to. Not my mother's face. Not the sound Uncle Ray's chair made when he shoved it back. The candies.

Grandma Evelyn used to steal three of them every time she visited Owen's office. She'd slip one into my coat pocket on the way out, wink, and whisper, "Don't tell the lawyer." As if Owen hadn't been refilling that bowl for her for thirty years.

There were exactly three candies in the bowl the morning we came to hear her will. I don't think that was an accident.

"Thank you all for coming," Owen said, adjusting his bow tie. It was navy with tiny ducks on it. Grandma had given it to him. "Evelyn asked that this be done quickly and plainly, so I'll honor that."

My mother sat to my left, straight-backed in a black wool dress and her good pearls. Diane Mercer-Bishop did not slouch, not at funerals, not at will readings, not ever. She hadn't looked at me since the cemetery.

Across from us sat Uncle Ray and his wife, Celeste. Ray had squeezed himself into a gray suit that had fit him maybe ten years ago, and he was tapping his wedding ring against the arm of his chair. Tap. Tap. Tap. Celeste had her phone face-down in her lap and her lipstick freshly done, like she had somewhere to be afterward. Knowing Celeste, she probably did.

And then there was me. Nora Bishop, thirty-eight, hospice nurse, still in the scrubs I'd worn on my overnight shift because I hadn't had time to go home and change. I'd tried to fix my hair in the car. It hadn't worked.

For the last three years, I had been the one who drove out to Grandma's house every morning before work. I sorted her pills into the little plastic days of the week. I sat with her through the bad nights when she called me by my mother's name. I was there at 4:12 a.m. on the Tuesday she died, holding her hand, her thin gold watch loose on her wrist.

That watch was on my wrist now. Nobody had asked me about it. I don't think anybody had noticed.

"To my daughter, Diane," Owen read, "I leave my mother's pearl brooch, my recipe box, and my love, which she has never needed to earn."

Mom's chin lifted a fraction of an inch. That was all.

"To my son, Raymond, I leave my late husband Walter's pocket watch and his fishing tackle, and my hope that he will someday sit by the water long enough to be still."

Ray let out a short laugh, the kind with no humor in it. "Cute, Mom," he muttered at the ceiling.

Owen turned a page. "To my granddaughter, Nora Bishop, I leave Mercer Hardware, including the building at 112 Main Street and all its inventory and accounts. I also leave Nora the house at Pine Hollow Lake and everything in it."

For a second, nobody moved.

I honestly thought I'd misheard him. The store was Grandma's whole life. She'd opened it in 1968 with a loan from her brother and a hand-painted sign that still hung over the door. Uncle Ray had run the books there for twenty years. Everyone in Ashford assumed the store would go to Ray. Ray assumed it most of all.

And the lake house. My whole childhood lived in that house. The dock where Grandma taught me to bait a hook. The screened porch where she taught me to knit and gave up after I knitted a scarf with a hole you could put your arm through. The attic I was never, ever allowed to go into.

"That's not right," Ray said.

"Ray," Celeste said quietly, touching his sleeve.

"No. That's not right." He was standing now, his chair scraping hard across the floorboards. "Owen, read it again."

"I read it correctly, Ray."

"She wasn't in her right mind. You know she wasn't. Half the time she didn't know what year it was." Ray jabbed a finger toward me without looking at me, like I was a stain on the carpet. "And who was with her every single day? Who was whispering in her ear?"

"I was taking care of her," I said. My voice came out smaller than I wanted it to. "Somebody had to."

"Oh, somebody had to," Ray mimicked. "Saint Nora. Changing bedpans for a lake house. That's a heck of an hourly rate."

"That's enough," Owen said.

But it wasn't enough, not for Ray, and I realized it wasn't enough for my mother either, because she finally turned and looked at me. I had seen that look exactly once before, the night I told her I was leaving Greg. Cold, careful, disappointed in a way that went all the way down to the bone.

"Did you know about this?" she asked.

"No. Mom, I swear, I had no idea."

"Three years," she said, mostly to herself. "Three years of you over there every morning. And I thought it was kindness."

That one landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

"It *was* kindness," I said. "You could have come. Any of you. She asked about you every single day. She asked when Ray was going to visit. She asked why you only called on Sundays."

"Don't you dare," Mom said, very quietly, "tell me about my own mother."

Celeste stood, gathering her purse. "We'll be speaking to our own attorney," she said to Owen, all polite steel. "I'm sure you understand. Evelyn had been declining for a long time. Anyone could see it."

"Evelyn signed this will in my presence, with two witnesses, and a letter from her physician confirming her capacity," Owen said. "But you're welcome to speak to whoever you like."

Ray leaned over the desk, close enough that Owen had to tilt his head back. "You were her friend for forty years," he said, low. "And you let this happen."

"I was her friend for forty years," Owen agreed. "That's precisely why I did."

Ray straightened up, buttoned his too-tight jacket, and looked at me for the first time since we'd sat down. Up close, his eyes were bloodshot. He smelled like coffee and something sharper underneath.

"Enjoy it while you can, kiddo," he said. "It's not going to last."

Then he walked out, and Celeste followed, heels clicking down the hallway. The front door of the building banged shut so hard the windows rattled.

My mother stayed in her chair another moment. She picked a piece of lint off her dress that wasn't there.

"Mom," I said. "Please. Can we just talk about this?"

She stood up, smoothed her skirt, and lifted her purse onto her shoulder.

"Congratulations, Nora," she said. "You always did want to be her favorite."

She didn't slam the door. She closed it very gently, which was so much worse.

And then it was just me and Owen and the bowl of butterscotch candies, and the ticking of the old clock on his bookshelf.

I put my face in my hands. I didn't cry. I was too tired to cry. I'd been awake for twenty-two hours, and somewhere in the last ten minutes I had become the villain of my own family.

"I don't want it," I said through my fingers. "Owen, give it to them. Give Ray the store. I'll sign whatever I have to sign."

"No," Owen said. "You won't."

I looked up. He had taken off his reading glasses and was folding them very slowly, the way he did when he was choosing his words.

"Your grandmother knew this would happen," he said. "Almost exactly this way. She told me Ray would stand up first, and your mother would leave last. She told me Celeste would mention a lawyer before she mentioned grief." He gave a tired little smile. "She was rarely wrong about her own children, Nora. She just loved them anyway."

"Then why would she do this to me?"

"She didn't do it *to* you." Owen opened his desk drawer and took out a cream-colored envelope. It was thick and slightly yellowed, the flap sealed with a blob of red wax. In the corner, in that familiar slanted handwriting, Grandma had written my name.

And underneath my name, a single line.

*Only after they've shown you who they are.*

My heart started beating hard enough that I could feel it in my throat.

"She gave me this two years ago," Owen said. "With very specific instructions. I was to hand it to you after the will was read, and only if the family reacted the way she expected." He held it out across the desk. "I'd say they have."

I took it. The envelope was heavier than it looked. Something small and hard shifted inside, sliding into one corner. A key, maybe. Or a coin.

"What is this?" I asked.

"I don't know what's inside," Owen said. "That's the truth. But I know Evelyn spent a long time deciding whether to write it." He paused. "And Nora? She asked me to tell you one more thing when I gave it to you."

"What?"

He reached into the glass bowl, took out one of the three butterscotch candies, and set it on the desk in front of me, the way Grandma used to.

"She said, 'Tell her not to open it at the house. Tell her someone will be watching.'"

I stared at him. Outside, through the window, I could see the parking lot. My old Honda. Owen's car.

And a gray pickup truck idling at the far end of the lot, engine running, with a man behind the wheel who was looking straight at Owen's office window.

At me.
