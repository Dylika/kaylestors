The first thing I noticed was his shoes.

Brown work boots with a strip of silver duct tape across the left toe. I'd seen those boots by our back door my whole life. I'd seen my mother wrap that tape around them at the kitchen table one Thanksgiving, laughing that he was too cheap to buy new ones.

They were the last thing I expected to see at 3:12 a.m. in Room 614, pushing a yellow cleaning cart through the doorway.

I looked up from the stripped mattress I was wiping down, and my father looked at me, and for a full second neither of us breathed.

"Nadia," he said. Just my name, the way you'd say it if you dropped a glass and watched it fall.

To understand why I nearly sat down on the floor, you need to know what I believed about my father up to that moment.

Six years ago, my mother, Rosalind, died of pancreatic cancer on the sixth floor of St. Bartholomew's Regional. 6 East, oncology. She was there for eleven weeks. I was nineteen, a sophomore failing chemistry because I couldn't concentrate, and the nurses on that floor were the only people who seemed to know what to say to me. One of them, a woman named Deb with reading glasses on a beaded chain, brought me hot chocolate from the staff room every night and told me I could cry in the linen closet whenever I needed to.

That's why I became a nurse. I switched majors that spring.

Nursing school was expensive. My father, Walt Keane, owned a small hardware store on Delancey Street, and after Mom died, he sold it. He told me it was time. He said he was tired, the big box store on Route 9 was eating him alive anyway, and between the sale and Mom's life insurance, he was "set." He told me not to worry about my loans.

"I've got them handled," he said. "Your mother would've wanted you to start clean."

Every few months I'd log into my loan account and see another large payment applied. Eighteen hundred. Twenty-two hundred. I'd call him, and he'd say, "Insurance money, kiddo. It's what it's for."

He told me he spent his days gardening and his nights sleeping like a rock. When I visited on weekends, he was often napping in his recliner at two in the afternoon. I thought that was what retirement looked like.

I graduated in May. I passed my boards in July. And in September, I accepted a night-shift position at St. Bartholomew's. On 6 East.

My dad got quiet when I told him. I assumed it was grief. Mom's floor.

"You sure you want to be there?" he asked.

"I want to be what Deb was for me," I said. "For somebody else."

He nodded and hugged me too long and didn't say anything else.

My first week was a blur of call lights, medication scanners, and the particular exhaustion of trying to look competent while being terrified. Deb was still there, now the night charge nurse, glasses still on their beaded chain. She recognized me instantly and hugged me in the hallway.

But odd things kept happening that I didn't think much about at the time.

My assigned rooms were always perfectly stocked. Gloves in every size, fresh linens folded in the cabinets, the sharps containers never full. The other new grad, a guy named Theo, complained constantly about hunting for supplies. I never had to.

On my second night, I found a granola bar in my locker. The peanut butter chocolate chip kind I'd eaten every day of high school. On my third night, there was a sticky note on the break room fridge with my name on it, stuck to a container of cut cantaloupe.

"Secret admirer," Theo teased.

I asked Deb. She just smiled and said, "This floor takes care of its own."

Then there was the traveling nurse, Bryce. He was on a thirteen-week contract and treated everyone below him like furniture. On my fourth night, I watched him snap at the overnight housekeeper, whom I'd only glimpsed from behind at the end of the hall, a gray-haired man in navy scrubs with his back to me.

"Hey. Janitor. I need 609 turned over in ten minutes, not twenty. Some of us have actual medical jobs."

The man said, "Yes, sir," in a low voice and kept working. I was at the far station charting and didn't see his face. I remember feeling a hot flash of embarrassment for Bryce, and not saying anything, which I'm not proud of.

Then came my fifth night.

A patient in 614 was transferred to the ICU around two in the morning, and the room needed a full terminal clean before an admission coming up from the ER. Environmental services was down a person. Deb found me between rounds.

"I hate to ask," she said, "but can you start stripping 614? Walt's covering two floors tonight. He'll be up to finish it."

Walt.

There had to be a hundred Walts in this city. I didn't even blink.

So I stripped the bed. I bagged the linens. I was spraying down the mattress when the yellow cart rolled in, and I saw the duct tape, and then I saw his face.

"Dad?"

He looked older under fluorescent light than he did in his recliner. The navy scrubs hung off his shoulders. His badge said Walter K. — Environmental Services, with a photo where he was smiling like he'd been told a joke.

"What are you doing here?" I whispered.

He glanced at the door, then back at me. "Cleaning room 614," he said, trying for a smile and missing.

"Dad."

He let out a long breath and pulled the door mostly closed.

"There wasn't any insurance money," he said quietly. "Your mother let the policy lapse when the store was struggling, before she got sick. She didn't tell me. I found out after." He rubbed his thumb along the cart's handle. "The store sale paid off her hospital bills. Most of them. There wasn't anything left."

"Then how..."

"Nights here. Six years now. Ten p.m. to six-thirty. I sleep in the afternoons." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Turns out I'm good at it. Mopping's not so different from sweeping out a hardware store."

I couldn't speak. I thought about every afternoon I'd found him asleep in that recliner and teased him about being an old man.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you'd have quit school," he said simply. "Or worked yourself sick picking up shifts. You were nineteen and you'd just lost your mother. You needed one thing in your life that wasn't hard." His voice cracked. "I could give you that one thing."

"The granola bars," I said slowly. "The cantaloupe. The supplies in my rooms."

His ears went pink. "I stock this floor anyway. Figured I'd stock it a little better in certain places."

"And you asked for 6 East. Six years ago."

He looked at the bed, the stripped mattress where some stranger had lain an hour before. Where, for all I knew, my mother had lain.

"First few months, I couldn't walk past her room," he said. "Then one night, a nurse was sitting in the linen closet crying, a young one, and I brought her some coffee. And I thought, this is where people need help at three in the morning. Same as we did." He swallowed. "It made me feel close to your mom. Doing something useful in the place where I couldn't do anything for her."

I put down the spray bottle and hugged him so hard the cart rolled into the wall.

When we came out of 614 forty minutes later, the room spotless, Bryce was waiting at the nurses' station, tapping his watch.

"About time," he said, looking right past me at my father. "Do you people get paid by the hour, or by how slow you go?"

Something in me that had stayed silent the night before stood up.

"His name is Walt," I said. "He's covering two floors tonight because you people are short-staffed. He stocks your rooms, he empties your sharps, and he's been on this floor longer than you've been a nurse." I took a breath. "He's also my father. So you can call him Walt, or Mr. Keane."

The station went completely quiet. Theo's mouth fell open. Behind Bryce, Deb took off her glasses and let them swing on their chain, the way she did when she was about to enjoy something.

"Bryce," she said pleasantly, "Walt here has been nominated by this unit for the hospital's Employee of the Year three years running. The nurses voted. I wrote the letters myself." She smiled. "And I'll be writing your contract evaluation this week."

Bryce went very pink and said, "Sorry, Walt," to the floor. He was noticeably polite to everyone for the rest of his contract.

But that wasn't the part that undid me.

At six-thirty, Dad and I walked to the parking garage together in the gray morning light. I told him I'd take over the loan payments, starting now. I'd pay him back every cent of what he'd put in.

He laughed, reached into his scrub pocket, and handed me a folded piece of paper.

It was a printout from my loan servicer. Date of final payment: September 8th.

The night before my first shift.

Remaining balance: $0.00.

"I wanted you to walk onto this floor owing nobody anything," he said. "That was the whole plan. I was going to put in my notice next week. You'd have thought I finally retired for real, and you'd never have known."

I stared at the paper until the numbers blurred.

"So you're done," I said. "You can quit."

He looked up at the sixth-floor windows, where the night lights were still glowing in the rooms.

"I've been thinking about that," he said. "I think I'll stay a while. Somebody's got to keep the new nurses in granola bars."

I still work nights on 6 East. Most shifts, around three in the morning, I hear the squeak of a yellow cart coming down the hallway, and I look up for a pair of brown boots with duct tape across the toe.

And on the fridge in the break room, there's still a sticky note with my name on it. Only now, I leave one back.
